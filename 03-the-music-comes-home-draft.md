## A note before you begin

This book is a builder's log, not a news report. A report says what happened. A log says what was decided, what was moved, and what comes next — and it keeps the receipts where the reader can check them.

Three rules govern every page that follows.

**First, the record leads.** Only verified facts appear as facts. Anything unverified is labeled as unverified. Anything unlocated is written "source not yet located" — never invented. Where a figure comes from inside an account page, this book says which page, and quotes the figure as the page recorded it. Where a figure comes from a message in Gmail, this book says so, and does not treat the message as the account. The DistroKid chapter of this story turns on exactly that distinction, and this book never blurs it.

**Second, his words are his.** When Chais speaks in this book, his words are quoted verbatim where they are used. When the book paraphrases him, it says so. When someone else — a model, a collaborator, a pasted document — said a thing, the thing stays with whoever said it. Cosmic titles and claims are attributed to whoever said them in the chats, never to him. This book is not his verbatim words. It is written from an outline, from named sources, in the voice of the record — and it says so here, so no reader mistakes the telling for a transcript.

**Third, a lead is not a conclusion.** This book contains one lead that matters — the capital-G versus lowercase-g artist spelling — and it spends a whole chapter explaining why a lead is worth following and why it is not yet a fact. The standard is the same everywhere in the book: follow the lead, check it, and write down what the check shows. Never upgrade a lead into a conclusion because the conclusion would be convenient.

### The frequencies in this book

One frequency fact in this book is stated as a public fact: the Vault's Founder's Seal is set at 963Hz, the Pure Origin Point. That is the one public frequency the record states as fact.

No other frequency appears in this book as established science. This is a book about distribution, publishing, and a catalog coming home. Frequencies do not belong in it except as that single stated fact, and this book does not import them from anywhere else.

### The methods in this book

Three methods run through this book, because they run through the work it describes. They are named here so you can watch for them in every chapter.

**The record-first method.** The record is kept before the story is told. The archive is updated first; the chapter follows. Before any move, walk the old account page by page and write down what is there. Look only. Change nothing. The record leads; the move follows.

**The look-only method.** When Chais signed in to the old account on 20 November 2026, he signed in himself, with his own Google sign-in and his own two-factor authentication, and the walk was look-only: 06:19–06:26 EST, nothing changed, canceled, paid, withdrawn, or updated. That discipline is the method in miniature. You do not touch a thing until the record of it is complete — because the moment you touch it, you can no longer prove what it was.

**The highway method.** Every site leads to a site. Nothing in ScrollVerse stands alone; each artifact points to the next, and the whole web is the point. The new home this book describes — Vydia, the Smart Links, the sync map — is a highway in that sense: one release's Smart Link leads to the next, and the catalog holds together because the links hold together.

### What this book deliberately leaves out

Three things are not in this book, by design, and the outline says so plainly.

No unverified song-theft claim appears anywhere in this book. No payment-processor dispute material and no held-money figure appear anywhere in this book. Nothing in this book is a token or NFT sale, mint, or pre-sale.

And one song is held, not released, and no chapter is built around releasing it: "What We Do" stays held by Chais's word alone, and this book records the hold and leaves the song where he put it. That is stated here, and again in Chapter 10, so the absence is on the record rather than silent.

# Chapter 1 — The Walk: Look Only, Change Nothing

Before any move, walk the old account page by page and write down what is there. That is the whole of this chapter's method, and it is the method of the whole book.

On 20 November 2026, Chais signed in to his DistroKid account himself — Google sign-in, his own two-factor authentication — and the walk that followed was look-only. The extraction that records it gives the time and the rule in the same breath: 06:19–06:26 EST, and nothing changed, canceled, paid, withdrawn, or updated. No card was updated. No email was updated. Nothing was touched.

The discipline matters more than it looks like it does. A catalog move is a moment when it is easy to start fixing things before you have finished seeing them — to update a card, to click a restore link, to dismiss a warning — and every one of those small fixes would have destroyed a piece of the record. The warning on the page, the balance in the bank, the list in My Music: all of them are evidence only while nobody has touched them. The look-only rule is what keeps evidence evidence.

The account was hillchais1@gmail.com, signed in through Google. The subscription it showed was Active — Ultimate Plan, five artists — renewing November 11, 2027. The plan page listed its annual prices: Musician $22.99, Musician Plus $39.99, Ultimate $89.99. And the account showed no amount due anywhere — not on the Account page, not in the Manage popup, not on the Plan page. No past-due balance. No next payment amount. Nothing due.

That absence is a fact, and it matters, because a figure from Gmail said otherwise. The $103.88 "due" figure from Gmail does not appear in the account. This book records both halves of that honestly: the Gmail figure exists, as a message; the account shows no amount due, as the account. A message is not the account, and the account is not the message, and a builder's log keeps them separate until one of them is confirmed by the other. As of the walk, they were not reconciled, and this book does not reconcile them by assertion.

Two warnings sat on every page of the account. The first: "Your music has been temporarily removed from streaming services. Seems your credit card was declined. Update your card to automatically restore your music." It came with a "Restore my music" link and an "Update your billing info" popup. The second: "An email we sent to hillchais1@gmail.com has bounced. Please update your email or dismiss this message."

Read those warnings together and you have the shape of the old home: the music removed, the card declined, the email bounced, and the account itself still Active, still renewing in a year's time. The subscription was alive. The music was not out. That contradiction is not resolved in this chapter, because it was not resolved in the walk. It is recorded, and the record is the point.

The walk also had a boundary the extraction states plainly: the detailed statement lives at the DistroKid bank details page — line by line, song, artist, service, month and year, amount, five hundred rows a page, grand total $399.52 — with filters and a download button. No statement file was downloaded. The totals were read from the pages; the line-by-line file was not taken. A later pass can take it, and the gaps section at the end of this draft says so. This draft does not pretend the file is in hand.

So the walk ends where it began: with a record, and with nothing changed. Seven minutes, look-only, signed in by the owner, every page read, nothing touched. That is Step one. Everything else in this book — the counted money, the vanished nine, the lead, the decision, the landing — is built on those seven minutes, because a move made from a complete record is a different thing from a move made from a feeling. The feeling came too, and it has its chapter. But the feeling came after the record, and the record came first.

# Chapter 2 — One Release Listed

Years of work, one release on the page. That is the fact this chapter sits with, and it sits with it before it explains anything, because the fact is the whole of the chapter.

Read that line again: "Woohoo, your first album!" A page that greets years of work as a first album is a page that has lost the work, or never held it. Either way, the greeting is part of the record, because it tells you what the account thought it was showing: a beginning, not a catalog.

"Figure me out" appears in the Bank details only in the "without earnings" list, not in Stats. It earned nothing in this account view, and it does not appear in the statistics. It is listed, and it is silent — present on the page, absent from the money.

And there is an open item the extraction flags that this book carries forward honestly: "Figure me out," seven tracks, has no prior record in the inventory. It is not a release anyone had counted before the walk. The question the extraction leaves open is the question this book leaves open: check whether it is already in Vydia, or whether it needs moving too. The book does not answer that question here, because the walk did not answer it. It is written down as an open item, and it stays open until someone checks.

It is worth pausing on what one release means. A catalog is a life's work in pieces — albums, singles, features, versions — and a distributor's account is supposed to be the shelf that holds them. When the shelf holds one release, the question is not only where the rest went. The question is what the shelf was ever holding, and whether the work was scattered somewhere the shelf could not show. A builder's log does not rush past a fact like that to get to the explanation. It writes the fact down, in full, and lets it stand. Years of work. One release on the page. That is the record, and the record is the chapter.

# Chapter 6 — The Lead: Capital G, Lowercase g

This chapter teaches the difference between a lead and a cause. It lays out exactly what would prove or disprove the lead, who would have to check it, and why the book does not call it settled.

The lead is a spelling. The nine older releases were credited to "Chais the Great" — capital G. All earnings and stats in this account view are under "Chais the great" — lowercase g — and "609Ball out." One letter, one case, and the music and the money never lined up.

The lead points to a possible duplicate artist profile: the nine may sit under a separate DistroKid artist profile, or under a different account altogether. That is the shape of the lead, and this book states its status in the same sentence every time it states the lead: **not confirmed.** The Source Index holds the same line, and this book holds it with it: "The duplicate-artist-profile lead (Ep 5) is a lead, not a cause. It stays UNCONFIRMED."

Here is what would prove it. DistroKid support, looking at the account from the inside, could confirm whether a second artist profile exists under the same account, or whether the nine releases live under a different account, or whether they were removed and why. The restore flow — the "Restore my music" link on the account's own warning — could bring the nine back into view, and the profile they returned under would answer the question by showing itself. Either check would settle it: the nine return under "Chais the Great," and the duplicate is real; or they return under "Chais the great," and the spelling was a display difference rather than a second profile; or they do not return, and the question becomes a removal question rather than a profile question.

Here is what would disprove it. If support confirms there is only one profile, and the nine were removed for another reason — the declined card, the bounced email, a takedown, a catalog action — then the capital G is a red herring, a difference in how the releases were entered rather than a difference in where they live. If the nine are found under a different account entirely, then the lead is not wrong exactly, but it is incomplete: the problem would be an account, not a profile. The book records these possibilities now, before any check is run, so that when a check is run, the result has somewhere honest to land.

Here is who would have to check it. DistroKid support, or Chais himself through the restore flow. Nobody else can see inside the account. The extraction that this book is built on was a look-only walk by the owner, and a look-only walk cannot see a second profile from the outside. The check is not a research task for a later chapter. It is a support conversation, or a restore click, and the decision to make it is Chais's — because restoring the music on DistroKid would work against the decision to leave, unless the nine need rescuing first. That tension is real, and it is the extraction's own open item, carried here in full: update the declined card and the bounced email only if he wants the music restored on DistroKid; he has already decided DistroKid is done and the music moves to Vydia, and restoring there would work against that decision unless the nine need rescuing first.

So the lead stands, unconfirmed, with its test named and its checker named. A builder follows a lead, checks it, and writes down what the check shows. This draft writes down the lead, the test, and the fact that the check has not yet been run. A later pass will record the answer, whatever it is — including the answer that the lead was wrong. A book that is willing to record being wrong is a book that can be trusted when it records being right.
