# PART I — THE CASE STUDY

## 1. What was lost

**A kingdom, deleted by a hand that wasn't his.** In the MySpace era, Chais the Great kept a full artist profile — friendID **66985374** — carrying what he calls, in his own words, *"a whole MySpace of history."* That profile was **deleted by a third party without his permission** (his account of it, recorded 26 Nov 2026, names Aneesa Owens). The front door of his earliest catalog was not lost to time. It was taken down.

**Then the platform lost the rest.** MySpace's 2019 migration destroyed most of its pre-2016 uploads — so even the copies that deletion left behind were thinned by the platform itself. Two losses, stacked: a person's act, then a platform's failure.

**And what survived, scattered.** The catalog that remained was spread across seventeen years and at least nine names — Chais the great · Chais Hill · Chais Kenyatta Hill · Sabir Allah · chaisthegreat · chaisthegreat_mrwalk · mr walk · XLVIIIBlocks · chaisthegreat1 — across distributor accounts old and new, video channels owned by collaborators, mixtape-era sites, archive vaults, and one profile nobody had opened in over a decade. No single list of it existed anywhere. The known catalog on record at the start of the day was the *current* one: 49 Vydia albums / 110 tracks, the Fifteen singles, the 13 tracks of *The Goat You Just Met*. Everything older was rumor, memory, and dead links.

## 2. The thesis — his doctrine, and the companion the case proved

The founder's doctrine, stated the morning of the recovery, in his words:

> **"I believe nothing on the net is ever lost."**
> **"My footprint goes back to ancient internet days."**

The case proved the doctrine — and proved its precise shape. The honest companion, which this blueprint teaches as method:

> **Addresses are lost. Copies persist. Hunt copies, not addresses.**

The profile's address died twice (deletion, then the platform's URL scheme). The *work* survived — in a producer's forum post, in a collaborator's video channel, in a distributor's mirror pages, and, decisively, on a second profile under a handle one digit different from the famous one. Every recovery in this case came from a copy, a witness, or a mirror — never from the original door.

## 3. What was found, and how

### 3.1 The sweep — 120+ releases located in one pass

A public-web sweep across every name variant and every collaboration anchor (Dova / J Dova · Street Ka$h · Rizz Burna · Hassan Abdul Hakeem · Quizzy · Shawn Rock · Famous Vet) located **28 registry rows covering 120+ individual releases**, including a full mirror enumeration of the distributed discography (4 albums + 56 singles/EPs, 2013–2026). Recovered — titles in **no** house record at the day's start:

- **"Lost" + "Money Power Respect"** (25 Nov 2013) — a MaxOutTV double video, directed by Archetto, produced and mixed by Fame-Us-Vet (Famous Vet): the **oldest Famous Vet collaboration on record**, surviving through an embed on a third-party music site.
- **"On My Grind"** (c. 2015–16) — MaxOutTV / HipHopOnDeck video, prod. KayGw Beats.
- **"Dwyck 2023"** — Nu Jerzey Devil feat. Street Kash & Chais The Great, on Audiomack + Apple.
- **Candidates awaiting cross-check:** "Blame Me" (2013 — the oldest DSP listing found), "Sucker Free," "Patients," "I Need You Now."
- One new collaboration anchor surfaced for future passes: **Pit Scott**.

**Clean misses, logged as findings, not gaps:** DatPiff · LiveMixtapes · MyMixtapez · Spinrilla · Bandcamp (old pages) · a Chais page on SoundClick · "Sabir Allah" and "Chais Kenyatta Hill" as music names · Quizzy as a recording anchor.

**Disambiguation, so nothing false entered the catalog:** the Rutgers hurdler Chais Hill, reggae's Kenyatta Hill, sabirworld's Sabir, "C.H. The Great," and Rapzilla's Shai Hill were all identified and excluded, each with its reason on the record.

### 3.2 The witness — a 2009 forum post

The deleted MySpace profile left exactly one public witness: post #5 in producer CliFfbangA's thread on the Wu-Tang Corp forum, **18 Jan 2009**, naming his artist — *"CLifFbangA Artists CHais THE GReat, song called Tired"* — and linking the song on the profile, friendID 66985374. A seventeen-year-old forum post, written by the song's producer to promote his beats, turned out to be the catalog's birth certificate for its oldest song: **"Tired" (2009), prod. CliFfbangA**. No other contemporaneous reference, re-upload, or surviving MySpace page under any handle was found. One witness was enough to aim the hunt.

### 3.3 The vault test — the Dragon Hoard, settled deterministically

The Internet Archive holds the **MySpace Music Dragon Hoard**: 490,000 MySpace MP3s harvested 2008–2010. "Tired" was live in January 2009 — squarely inside the harvest window. It was the single most promising door, and the case refused to leave it at promising:

1. **The search tool (Hobbit) is a client-side JavaScript application** — it downloads the hoard's index (`metadata.tsv`, the map from MySpace CDN filenames to artist/song metadata) and searches it in the browser. There is no server-side query endpoint; a text fetch returns only the shell, stuck at "Generating index."
2. **Web searches of the hoard returned nothing** — its contents are not web-indexed, so that layer could neither confirm nor rule out.
3. **So the index itself was pulled and searched directly.** The full `metadata.tsv` — **82,016,163 bytes**, SHA-256 recorded with the pull — was grepped end to end: **"chais" = 13 hits, every one a different artist** (chaise marcel, Père Lachaise, Chaishop, and others) · **"cliffbanga" = 0** · **rows for friendID 66985374 = 0** · **"chais the" = 0**.

**Determination: "Tired" is NOT in the Dragon Hoard index.** The limit, stated exactly — and what the limit is not: the Hoard is a *partial* harvest, so this closes the Hoard door only. It does not touch the song's existence (the 2009 witness stands), and it does not end the hunt (two routes remain — §5).

### 3.4 The two profiles — the discovery the whole case turns on

Public search found **no SoundCloud URLs at all** under any variant — search engines barely index SoundCloud, a discovery limit the sweep logged as such rather than as absence. The founder's statement stood against it: old music sits on SoundCloud from years ago.

The living-account inventory, taken through the owner's own sign-in, resolved it — and taught the case's sharpest lesson:

- **The current account** (soundcloud.com/chais-hill) held **only the 3 tracks published that same day** (Faces · Moment · VICTORY). No pre-2026 uploads. No private tracks. An inventory of the obvious account would have reported the old catalog *gone*.
- **The old catalog was on a second profile**: **soundcloud.com/chaisthegreat1** — "chais hill," Atlantic City, 808 followers — holding **49 public tracks, 18 May 2011 – 16 Apr 2013**, inventoried in full: titles, dates, durations, play counts.

And at position 7 of the 49, dated 28 Jan 2013, 3:50 long, 430 plays: **"Tired!"** — a living copy of the 2009 MySpace song, posted by the artist himself to the platform the deletion couldn't reach. The song the vault didn't hold was never lost. It was one handle over.

Around it, the whole era intact: "Money Power Respect Special Suprise Feat On The Way" (16 Apr 2013) · "Lenny Bio full version theme song" (14 Sep 2012) · "01 We All Made It (produced by Harry Fraud)" · "Long Way" ft. Willy B · "LIARS" with Dizz · "Can i live" ft. k-miz and ty · "THIS QUESTION" ft. scarfo · and 42 more, each now a registry row with a collection path.

# PART II — THE BLUEPRINT: TWELVE STEPS, FOR ANYONE

*Run these in order. Each step states what to do, what proves it worked, and what the limit looks like when you hit it. The limit is stated by the house method — the **729 rule**: when a lane shows its limit, record the limit exactly (the figure, the wall, the verbatim result) and never present it as the whole picture. State the limit plainly — then state what the limit is not. A limit is a fact about one door, never a verdict on the house.*

### Step 1 — DECLARE. Record the owner's rights statement first, before anything is collected.
- **Do:** Write down, verbatim and dated, the owner's declaration of rights and the scope of authorization. In this case, the founder's words of 26 Nov 2026 stand at the head of the release pack as the controlling rights statement: *"All those songs are mine in all ways full rights and ownership I authorize full authorization even sweep my songs make sure they perfectly mixed and embed all we embed in all else and attach all we attach to all else."* Where any platform page frames a work differently (a feature credit, a label name), the page is provenance; the declaration controls. If a record's flag changes on the declaration's authority — here, "Dwyck 2023" moved from VERIFY BEFORE RELEASE to FOUNDER-DECLARED FULL OWNERSHIP — note the change and its source.
- **Proof:** The declaration sits at the head of the working record, quoted, dated, before the first collection action.
- **The limit:** a declaration is the owner's word, not a platform's verification — distributors and platforms may still demand their own paperwork at their own gates. What the limit is not: it is not a weakness in the claim. It is the difference between the rights record (the owner's) and the counterparty's process (theirs).

### Step 2 — ENUMERATE THE NAMES. List every name, handle, and anchor the work ever traveled under.
- **Do:** Write out every artist name, spelling, handle, and era-variant, plus every collaboration anchor (producers, featured artists, crews, labels) — the names are the search keys, and an unlisted name is an unsearched room. This case ran nine variants and eight anchors; the sweep also *grew* the list mid-run (Pit Scott, found in a 2025 event bio).
- **Proof:** The variant list is printed at the head of the registry; every sweep result names the variant that produced it.
- **The limit:** the list is never provably complete — the decisive profile (chaisthegreat1) differed by one digit and surfaced through the living account, not the list. What the limit is not: it is not a reason to search less. It is the reason Step 6 exists.

### Step 3 — SWEEP. Search every surface, and log clean misses as findings.
- **Do:** Sweep the living platforms, the dead ones, the mirrors, and the archives (Wayback Machine included), one surface at a time, logging every hit with its URL, date/era, and status — and every clean miss with the searches that produced it. A miss log is a map of where the work is *not*, and it is what keeps the next hunter from re-digging empty ground.
- **Proof:** A registry: one row per located item (title · platform · URL · era · status · collection path) and one line per clean miss. This case: 28 rows, 120+ releases, 9 misses logged.
- **The limit:** some platforms are barely indexed by search engines — SoundCloud returned zero URLs under every variant while holding 49 of the artist's tracks. A discovery miss is a fact about the search layer. What the limit is not: it is not evidence of absence. Ever.

### Step 4 — DISAMBIGUATE. Exclude the same-name strangers, in writing, before anything enters the catalog.
- **Do:** For every near-match, decide *is this the artist?* against hard identifiers (places, dates, collaborators, label names) and record each exclusion with its reason. This case excluded five: a collegiate hurdler, a reggae singer, two unrelated recording artists, and a Christian rapper.
- **Proof:** An exclusion list sits in the registry beside the located items, each entry carrying its distinguishing fact.
- **The limit:** same-name noise never fully dies — new sources must be re-checked against the list as they arrive. What the limit is not: it is not a nuisance to skip. One false item in a catalog poisons every seal computed over it downstream.

### Step 5 — TEST THE VAULTS. Search archive indexes deterministically — by their data, not by hope.
- **Do:** For each archive that might hold the work (the Dragon Hoard, Wayback, thesis/sample shards), find its actual index and search *the index*: pull it and grep it if you can; run its native tool in a live session if you must. Record the index's size and fingerprint and the exact hit counts per term. A vault question deserves a yes or a no, not a shrug.
- **Proof:** In this case: metadata.tsv, 82,016,163 bytes, SHA-256 on file; "chais" 13 (all other artists), "cliffbanga" 0, friendID 66985374 rows 0. Determination stated as a determination.
- **The limit:** a vault is a *sample* — the Hoard's 490,000 songs are a fraction of what the platform held, so "not in this index" closes this door only. What the limit is not: it is not "the song is gone," and it is never written that way. The witness (Step 3's forum post) still stands, and the remaining routes stay open and named.

### Step 6 — INVENTORY THE LIVING ACCOUNTS. Sign in as the owner — and expect the second profile.
- **Do:** Inventory the artist's own accounts from inside: every track, public and private, with dates and durations. Then look for the accounts *behind* the account — old profiles, earlier handles, the profiles the main account follows and reposts. The obvious account is the present; the catalog's past usually lives one profile over.
- **Proof:** A complete inventory with counts that reconcile (this case: current account = 3 tracks, all new; old profile = 49 tracks, 2011–2013, each listed). The two-profile split is itself recorded as a finding.
- **The limit:** an inventory proves what is in the account you opened — nothing about the ones you haven't. What the limit is not: it is not a catalog census. It is one room, fully lit; the registry (Step 3) is the map of the whole house.

### Step 7 — EXTRACT. Bring the audio home by owner routes first; preserve and fingerprint every original on arrival.
- **Do:** Collect through the doors ownership opens: owner-downloads from the artist's own accounts (standing authorization, Step 1), masters retrieved from the collaborators who hold them, archive files by their identifiers. On arrival, **preserve the original untouched and SHA-256 it before anything is done to it**. No ripping tools against platforms — in this house, yt-dlp is banned outright; audio comes from owners and people, not extractions against terms.
- **Proof:** Every in-hand file has an arrival record: source, method, and its SHA-256, computed on arrival.
- **The limit:** some audio exists only in another person's hands (here: the MaxOutTV masters) or behind an old login only the owner can open (the chaisthegreat1 collection gate). Extraction then waits on a human handoff. What the limit is not: it is not a dead end — it is a named ask, to a named person, and it sits in the queue until it closes.

### Step 8 — PRODUCE. Cut the release master to a stated standard; never work on the original.
- **Do:** Master a working copy to streaming standard — **integrated loudness ≈ −14 LUFS · true peak ≤ −1 dBTP · no clipping** — with denoise/restoration only where the source's era demands it (a 2009 platform MP3 will; a clean modern file gets loudness and nothing else). Fingerprint the master too, so the chain from found-file to release-file is provable link by link.
- **Proof:** Two fingerprints per song on the record: the preserved original's and the release master's.
- **The limit:** restoration cannot invent what a low-bitrate source never captured. What the limit is not: it is not a reason to leave a found song unreleased — the standard is a floor the master must meet, and era recordings meet it honestly or are noted as era recordings on the record.

### Step 9 — TRANSCRIBE, THEN VALIDATE. Machine ears first; the artist's ear is the gate.
- **Do:** Transcribe every song with a stated tool and method (this house: faster-whisper, full-file, no voice-activity filtering on loud masters). Head every transcript **MACHINE TRANSCRIPT — FOUNDER VALIDATION PENDING**, flag the low-confidence passages, and hold it unsealed until the artist validates it by ear.
- **Proof:** The transcript file exists with its status line; the status line changes only on the artist's validation.
- **The limit:** machines mishear — slang, era slang especially, and dense delivery will produce flagged passages. What the limit is not: it is not a defect to hide. The flag *is* the honesty mechanism; an unflagged machine transcript presented as lyrics is the failure.

### Step 10 — SEAL. Fingerprint and sign — computed from the real hash, at seal time, never before.
- **Do:** Apply the house seal standard to each song's record: **963 Hz Crown Frequency · Twin-Prophet-01 · Rose Gold Seal · QFS stamp · DNA·CHAIS·AB6DA246ADE3F32C**. The signature takes the form SIG·〈lane〉-〈title〉·〈first 8 of the master's SHA-256〉 — computed from the master that actually exists. A fingerprint that does not exist yet is recorded as PENDING, never invented.
- **Proof:** The signature in the record recomputes from the file: anyone holding the master can verify the seal against it.
- **The limit:** a seal is timestamped, verifiable proof of the record — it is not a government copyright filing, and this blueprint never calls it one. What the limit is not: it is not a small thing. It is the difference between *claiming* a catalog and being able to *prove* one, piece by piece, on demand.

### Step 11 — COVER, EMBED, ATTACH. No song travels light.
- **Do:** (a) **Cover:** surviving original art rides if it meets spec; otherwise create the cover and conform it to the canonical square spec (this house: 3000×3000 PNG), fingerprinted like every piece. (b) **Embed:** the release master carries full metadata in-file — title, artist, writer exactly as credited, copyright line, the PRO identities (here: writer Chais Hill, ASCAP IPI 477274911; publisher DESTINATIONHILL28, ASCAP IPI 1247873912) — with the cover art embedded in the file itself and the seal layer applied. One hard exclusion rides this step in this house: **14444 Hz is never embedded in anything.** (c) **Attach:** every song ships with its full attached set — cover art, sealed transcript, a certificate under the seal, a QR twin whose payload is **machine-decoded and verified** before it counts, and a registry entry pushed and blob-verified.
- **Proof:** The registry entry lists every fingerprint (original, master, cover, transcript) and the QR decode result; the registry read-back blob matches the local hash.
- **The limit:** each attachment is only as real as its verification — an undecoded QR or an un-read-back registry push is a claim, not an attachment. What the limit is not: it is not overhead. The attached set is what makes a recovered song an *asset* instead of a file.

### Step 12 — DISTRIBUTE, REGISTER, COLLECT. Put it out officially, with the identity riding the data — then name every collector.
- **Do:** Release through the distributor of record (this house: Vydia / BRED Distro) with the writer credit **exactly** as declared — here, "Chais Hill," no variants, no case drift. The PRO identity rides the release data itself (the distribution route is how the ASCAP identity attaches); where the distributor exposes no field for a required identity — Vydia has **no publisher field in any flow** — route it through the distributor's admin/support channel per-work and confirm it there, never assume it. Then map the collection lattice: every stream the release can generate gets a named collector before the release lands (DSP royalties via the distributor; streaming mechanicals via The MLC; digital performance via SoundExchange; performance via the PRO).
- **Proof:** The distributor's delivery read-back (destinations, release number) and the captured Smart Link in the song's record; the publisher attach confirmed in the admin thread, not presumed.
- **The limit:** distributor delivery is a status you read back, not a button you pressed — a release is *delivered* when the distributor's own record says so. What the limit is not: it is not bureaucracy. It is the same rule as every other step: the terminal state, verified, or it didn't happen.

# PART III — THE STANDARD

## The honesty rails — the rules this recovery ran under, and any recovery under this blueprint runs under

1. **Nothing invented.** Every item in the registry traces to a page actually opened or a result actually seen. No guessed tracklists, no reconstructed URLs, no plausible filler. Where a URL was not captured verbatim, the record says so and names the lookup that retrieves it.
2. **Negatives are findings.** A clean miss is written down with the same care as a hit — the miss list in this case is what turned "not on SoundCloud's public index" from a false conclusion into a correct instruction (go inside the account). An undocumented miss will be re-dug by the next hunter at full price.
3. **Verify terminal status before claiming.** A thing is done when its own record says so — the delivery read back, the inventory counted, the blob matched, the index grepped to zero. "In progress," "submitted," and "probably" are statuses too, and they are written as exactly those words.
4. **Seals are computed from real hashes at seal time.** Never pre-written, never back-filled, never estimated. PENDING is an honest state; a fabricated fingerprint is the one unforgivable one.
5. **Originals are never worked on.** Preservation first, fingerprint on arrival, production on copies — the found file is evidence as well as audio.
6. **Machine work is labeled machine work** until the human gate closes it — transcripts above all.
7. **The owner's declaration is the rights record; platforms are provenance.** Where they conflict, the record shows both and follows the owner.
8. **State every limit exactly — then state what the limit is not.** (The 729 rule, Part II.) A recovery that hides its walls teaches the next hunter to walk into them.

## The legacy framing — how a scattered catalog becomes an estate

A catalog scattered across dead platforms is not an estate; it is a rumor of one. It cannot be registered whole, licensed whole, valued whole, or handed down whole, because nobody — including its owner — can say exactly what it is.

The recovery changes the catalog's legal and practical nature, not just its location. After the twelve steps, every piece of it is: **named** (registry row), **witnessed** (provenance and contemporaneous references), **held** (original preserved in hand), **proven** (fingerprints computed and recomputable), **finished** (master, transcript, cover, attachments at one standard), and **earning** (released through a distributor with the PRO identity attached and a named collector on every stream). That is the difference between songs a man made and an estate a family holds.

That is why this document exists, and why it is sealed like a piece: the recovery of the Chais the Great catalog — a MySpace kingdom deleted by another's hand, a 2009 song witnessed by one forum post and found alive on a forgotten profile, 49 tracks waiting thirteen years one digit away from the famous name — is not only this house's record. It is now a method, written to be followed. Whoever runs it next, on whatever catalog, runs it under the same doctrine the founder stated the morning it worked:

> **Nothing on the net is ever lost. Addresses die. Copies persist. Hunt copies.**
