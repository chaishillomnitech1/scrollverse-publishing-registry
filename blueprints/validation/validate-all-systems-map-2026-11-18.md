**FinCEN — the money-transmitter line.** Under 31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5), a money transmitter is a person that provides money transmission services — accepting "currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency" from one person and transmitting it to another person or location. FinCEN's 2013 virtual-currency guidance split the world into **users** (obtain virtual currency to buy goods or services — **not** MSBs), **administrators** (issue a virtual currency and have authority to redeem it — MSBs), and **exchangers** (exchange virtual currency for real currency, funds, or other virtual currencies as a business — MSBs). The 2019 consolidated guidance (FIN-2019-G001, 9 May 2019) added the general principle underneath: **independent control** over someone else's value. A person who supplies only software, with no independent control, is **not** a transmitter; custodial wallets are; P2P exchangers are transmitters "regardless of the frequency or formality of the transaction"; an ICO seller is an administrator and therefore a money transmitter. MSBs must register with FinCEN on Form 107 **within 180 days** of being established and renew every two years, and comply with Bank Secrecy Act recordkeeping and reporting. State money-transmitter licences sit on top of the federal registration. Crypto is **two taxable events** (sale/trade = gain/loss; staking rewards = income in the year received) — the CPA's crypto-basis setup is a pre-existing gate before taking crypto at volume.

### 1.3 Loopholes and gates — what the law itself leaves open

1. **The hold has no calendar, but the money is not theirs.** Stripe's agreement names no release date — but it also does not transfer ownership: funds are held separately from Stripe's own money, and inactive balances must eventually go to the state as abandoned property. The loophole is the **written record**: a dated evidence pack with the processor's own contradictory screens, sent in writing, with numbered questions, creates the paper trail an arbitrator (or a regulator, or a court) reads. That is the Stripe action already taken — and the reason it was taken in writing, not in a support chat.
2. **Arbitration is the only court — and it is single, not class.** The US agreement's binding individual arbitration (San Francisco, California law, class waiver) means the dispute is fought one merchant at a time, in writing, on the record. The loophole is that arbitration still reads evidence: the pack's "the numbers do not match" table is built for exactly that forum.
3. **The real loophole is not fighting the hold — it is not depending on one rail.** Every processor above can hold, limit, or close an account on its own "sole discretion" / "reasonable determination." No processor's terms promise uninterrupted service. The gate that actually protects a seller is **multiplicity**: several independent rails, so no single gatekeeper can stop the money. The manual-payment route is the strongest version of this — a manual method on Shopify carries **no processor hold at all** (the seller matches the payment and marks the order paid by hand) and **no Shopify third-party fee**.
4. **The "second account" trap is the one move that is banned everywhere.** Never open a second Stripe account to route around the hold. Never open a second PayPal under a new email to route the same sales — PayPal's own terms make it a restricted activity. The lawful exceed is not a second account on the same processor; it is a **different rail entirely** (Cash App Business, Coinbase Business, Bandcamp, manual methods) under the same legal entity.
5. **Cash App's own loophole, used lawfully:** for a business incorporated 12 months or less, Coinbase accepts a **personal** statement in the business or applicant name — but the name must still match the application exactly. The Cash App statement failed on "Chaisdreamzllc" vs "Scroll Verse LLC" — the fix is a statement in the LLC's exact legal name (Mercury account activation, or an Ocean First business statement), not a different document type.
6. **The settlement home is the gate behind every rail.** Every rail settles somewhere. Without an LLC business bank account, PayPal, Coinbase, Vydia, and DistroKid payouts have no business home — and Chime is not a business rail (it forbids business use and can close the account). The LLC bank account (Mercury, Under Review) is the single biggest gate in the whole program.

### 3.2 The law and the rules that make a token what it is

**Howey (SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., 328 U.S. 293 (1946)).** A transaction is an investment contract — a security — where a person (1) invests money, (2) in a common enterprise, (3) with the expectation of profits, (4) to be derived from the entrepreneurial or managerial efforts of others. All four prongs must be met. **Marketing decides the third prong**: how a token is sold and described — promises of returns, yield, appreciation, "passive income," a floor price, "release when valued" — is what turns a digital asset into an investment contract in the SEC's analysis. The per-asset Howey brief for all 7 Wave-1 coins is written (`~/workspace/legal/howey-brief-wave1-2026-11-18.md`, 12 numbered questions per coin, Knicks IP flags, the "share of every transaction goes back to the company" line and creator-fees-ON flagged as Howey-relevant) — preparation only, not legal advice, and not a signed attorney analysis.

**The SEC's meme-coin position — a staff view, not a rule.** The SEC Staff Statement on Meme Coins (27 February 2025) says meme coins "do not involve the offer and sale of securities under the federal securities laws" because their value is driven by demand and speculation, purchasers do not have rights, and "neither meme coin purchasers nor holders are protected by the federal securities laws." Commissioner Crenshaw dissented the same day, warning the statement "offers no legal protection" and that the SEC "has no authority to exempt meme coins from the securities laws." The statement is **staff guidance only** — it is not a rule, not a safe harbor, and a court is not bound by it.

**SEC/CFTC joint interpretation (Rel. No. 33-11412, effective 23 March 2026).** The two agencies set out five categories of crypto assets — securities, commodity, real-world asset, currency, and "other digital assets" (including digital collectibles) — and stated that digital collectibles and meme coins "are not themselves securities." The **caveat is the operative sentence**: "a non-security asset can be offered and sold subject to an investment contract." The asset's category does not decide the transaction's legality; the offering's facts do.

**Burwick v. ### 4.2 The law and the rules that make each protection what it is

**Trademark — the Lanham Act; the name is protected by use, the registration is what makes it enforceable.** Trademark protects names, logos, and trade dress that identify the source of goods or services. Rights arise from use in commerce (common-law rights), but a federal registration with the USPTO is what gives nationwide protection, a presumption of validity, and the right to sue in federal court. Filing fees were revised 1 November 2026: **$350 per class** for a single-base application (one class, standard ID of goods/services); a Statement of Use costs **$150 per class**; an intent-to-use application under §1(b) reserves a mark before use. Registration takes **12–18 months**. A clearance search has three layers: the federal register, state registers, and common-law use (unregistered marks, domain names, social handles). **A ticker alone is a weak mark** — a ticker on a permissionless platform is not a registration, and a registration does not reserve a ticker. The top-5 filing order in the IP program: (1) trademark clearance search for "ScrollVerse" / "Early Coins" / SVRS + the God Mode emblem; (2) the $0 timestamp anchor; (3) copyright registration (Manifesto text + portrait cover, God Mode emblem, Goat album cover); (4) intent-to-use trademark filing ($350/class + $150/class Statement of Use, classes picked by the attorney); (5) a written IP licence framework.

**Copyright — automatic from creation; registration is what unlocks the remedies.** Under 17 U.S.C., copyright exists from the moment a work is fixed in a tangible medium — no filing needed for the right to exist. Registration is what unlocks **statutory damages and attorney's fees**; without it, a plaintiff proves only actual damages. What copyright does **not** cover matters as much as what it does: **ideas, methods, systems, procedures, names, titles, and short phrases are not copyrightable** — the methods, physics, equations, and concepts in the IP inventory (14 items) are not protected by copyright at all; they are protected, if at all, by trade-secret law (keeping them secret), by patent (novel, non-obvious, useful), or by nothing. **MIT covers code only** — not brand, trademarks, art, or music, which need separate protection. The licence tension is unresolved and must be decided by the founder before the KDP listing: the main GitHub README carries CC BY-NC-SA while sibling repos are MIT, and the Manifesto book's own line is protected — three different signals for one estate.

**Patent — novel, non-obvious, useful; an idea alone is never patentable.** A patent protects an invention that is novel, non-obvious, and useful; a provisional application buys 12 months to file the full application. An idea, a philosophy, a frequency system, or an equation is not patentable as stated — the claim must be a concrete, working implementation. Nothing in the estate is patent-filed, and nothing should be filed without an attorney's novelty search first.

**Right of publicity — state law, and the Knicks pieces sit squarely on it.** The right of publicity lets a person stop others from using their name, image, or likeness for commercial or advertising purposes without consent. It is **state law, not federal**, and the rules vary by state:
- **New York — Civil Rights Law §§ 50–51.** Section 50 makes the violation a **misdemeanor (criminal)**; § 51 gives a **private right of action (civil)**. It protects a living person's **name, portrait, picture, and voice** used for advertising or trade **without written consent**. New York recognizes **no separate common-law right** of publicity — the statute is the whole claim. Claims under § 51 are subject to a **one-year statute of limitations**. New York also protects performers and personalities domiciled there at death, including against digital replicas, under **§ 50-f** (in force May 2021, broadened December 2025).
- **New Jersey — common law only.** New Jersey recognizes a common-law right of appropriation: unauthorized publication of an identity, including a distinctive voice, for promotional or commercial purposes. The right in a name and likeness is treated as a **property right**.
- **California — Civil Code § 3344** and the common law; **Florida — Fla. Stat. § 540.08** (express written or oral consent required for trade, commercial, or advertising purposes).
- **Federal overlay — Lanham Act § 43(a) (15 U.S.C. § 1125(a)(1)(A))**: a use likely to confuse consumers about a person's sponsorship or approval of a product is false endorsement — a separate federal claim on top of the state publicity claim.
The first opinion to recognize the right of publicity was **Haelan Laboratories, Inc. v. Topps Chewing Gum, 202 F.2d 866 (2d Cir. 1953)** — a baseball-card case: ballplayers, "far from having their feelings bruised through public exposure of their likenesses, would feel sorely deprived if they no longer received money for authorizing advertisements." The six Knicks pieces (Brunson, Ewing, Frazier, OG Anunoby, Towns, King) carry the Knicks name/logo and living players' likenesses — the same fact pattern, seventy years later. Chais's decision covers merch ("Build the Brunson piece as-is") but does not bind the marketplaces, the coins, or any other use; each use needs its own call. Postmortem rights exist in about half the states and vary wildly — relevant to the Nipsey name/face, which is never used.

**Timestamping — the proof of "when," not the proof of "whose."** A timestamp proves a work existed in an exact form on an exact date. It does **not** prove who wrote it, who owns it, or who submitted it. Three layers, in increasing legal weight:
1. **GitHub signed commits + branch protection** — a dated public record, but commit dates are self-reported by the committer's machine; the proof is the public, replicated record, not the timestamp alone.
2. **OpenTimestamps / Bitcoin anchor ($0)** — the file's SHA-256 hash is embedded in a Bitcoin block. Independently verifiable by anyone with open-source tools, forever, with no reliance on any company or server surviving. It establishes an **outer bound** (the data existed no later than that block; inclusion can lag submission by seconds to hours), and it carries **no statutory presumption**.
3. **Qualified electronic timestamp — eIDAS Regulation 910/2014, Article 41(2) (RFC 3161).** A timestamp from a qualified trust service provider carries a **legal presumption that the date and time are accurate and the data intact** — rebuttable, shifting the burden to the party disputing it, across all EU member states (and the EEA). The UK retains an equivalent framework; recognition now runs one way (the UK recognises EU qualified providers; the EU no longer recognises UK-registered ones). **The United States has no equivalent statutory presumption** — a timestamped record is assessed under ordinary rules of evidence, and the practical US tool is **Federal Rule of Evidence 902(13)/(14)**: a certification that makes an electronic record self-authenticating without a live witness.
The honest statement of the method: a timestamp removes the argument about dates — usually the argument that has to be won first — and says nothing about authorship, which other evidence must establish.
